# Dog guardian — four logo directions

Prepared October 19, 2026. Exploration brief following the owner's dog-from-behind/keyhole idea and positive response to the playful tone.

**Shared idea:** a dog seen from behind, its tail raised or curved aside, with a traditional keyhole directly beneath the tail. The dog is the guardian; the keyhole supplies the visual joke and the vault connection.

Working name for optional typography tests: **WagLock**. Working slogan: **All wag. No tell.** Neither is a confirmed product rename or approved replacement for the repository's current Vaultito identity/tagline. Keep the symbol usable independently of wording. No app assets, installed identity, data or license notices change under this brief.

## Common design rules

- Friendly, mischievous and carefully drawn. The humor should come from the placement of the keyhole, without extra anatomical marks or props explaining the joke.
- Raised tail leaves the whole keyhole unobstructed. Avoid a tail that accidentally reads as a flame, plume or separate object.
- Traditional keyhole: one circular opening joining a short tapered stem. It must read as a keyhole, rather than a dot, exclamation mark or hanging padlock. Avoid any extra dot beneath it.
- Favor a compact, sturdy dog over long legs or a large torso. Use a generic original character; don't imitate a known cartoon dog or movie adaptation.
- No collar text, bones, shields, keys, sparkles, tiny fur lines, decorative lettering or tagline inside the app icon.
- Retain a full-color version and design a one-color version from the beginning. Color must not be the only way to identify the keyhole.
- For the initial comparison, use the same plum background and warm dog colors so composition can be compared fairly. Palette changes come after the drawing works.
- Keep art opaque and square for app-icon PNG exports; display iOS masks only in previews. Use transparent symbol exports for logo/login applications.

## 01 — Little Guardian

**Idea:** the straightforward mascot, refining the existing simplified sketch.

**Composition:** a complete dog from behind, with a compact head, two floppy ears, a rounded body, short back paws and a tail sweeping up to one side. The rear is the widest part. Place the keyhole on a simple cream patch beneath the tail. Use enough head/ear silhouette to establish “dog,” without showing a face.

**Drawing:** flat illustration with roughly six to eight major shapes, rounded contours and very few interior lines. No drawn toes at small sizes. One darker plane may separate the ears or tail from the body. Try a lower, shorter tail than the existing sketch to reduce its visual dominance.

**Color:** apricot dog, cream patch and tail tip, plum keyhole/background. Avoid glossy highlights and textured fur.

**Wordmark:** a sturdy rounded sans serif, with a normal readable lowercase sequence after the initial capital. Candidate Manrope weight 650–750. Put the mascot beside the wordmark with ample separation, not inside a letter. The optional slogan sits below the lettering.

**At small sizes:** simplify ear/body overlaps and paws; preserve the tail and keyhole. The test is whether the silhouette reads as a dog before the viewer studies it.

**What could go wrong:** too much body or head pushes the joke into a small detail. Excessively soft rendering makes the logo feel like a generic pet-care app.

**Why explore it:** closest to the sketch that made the owner laugh; easiest starting point for a recognizable recurring character.

## 02 — Cheeky Close-Up

**Idea:** make the rear and tail the entire mark.

**Composition:** crop the dog at the lower back, excluding its head and ears. Two rounded haunches, two short paw suggestions and one hooked tail fill the icon. Put the keyhole in the central negative space. The outline should have an uneven top edge from the tail and a stable, broad base.

**Drawing:** bolder and more graphic than Little Guardian, using three or four main masses. Give the outer haunches a distinctive contour; don't rely on a generic heart shape. Start with a medium-length curved tail rather than a breed-specific tail.

**Color:** cream/apricot two-tone rump on plum, with the keyhole cut through to the background. A cream-on-plum one-color version should keep the same reading.

**Wordmark:** visually quieter lettering so the close-up carries the personality. Use the symbol as a compact square companion; don't stretch it to match text height.

**At small sizes:** the larger relative keyhole should be the clearest of the four directions. Test whether the absent head makes it read as “dog,” “peach” or “moustache.” If most viewers need the explanation, revise the silhouette.

**What could go wrong:** it becomes only a bottom joke, losing the guardian idea. Keep enough tail/paw information to anchor the dog reading.

**Why explore it:** most direct joke and potentially the most economical app icon.

## 03 — Caught You Looking

**Idea:** a dog that knows exactly what the viewer has noticed.

**Composition:** rear three-quarter view, with the dog glancing back over one shoulder. Show one floppy ear, one eye and a small nose. Its body remains rear-facing; its tail curves to the opposite side of its face so neither competes with the central keyhole.

**Drawing:** an editorial cartoon with broad flat fills and a consistent heavy outline. The expression is calm and mildly knowing, rather than a broad grin or an aggressive guard-dog snarl. Pose and eye direction carry the personality. Avoid eyebrows, teeth and tongue detail.

**Color:** apricot with cream haunch patch, plum outline and keyhole. Keep the face readable through silhouette rather than tiny white highlights.

**Wordmark:** friendly and upright. Let the mascot appear to glance toward the wordmark in a horizontal lockup. Do not mirror the face casually between exports: choose a consistent pose.

**At small sizes:** the face should reduce to one clear eye and nose. If it competes with or obscures the keyhole at 40 px, treat this as a larger website/onboarding illustration and use a simpler primary icon.

**What could go wrong:** the character looks surprised, embarrassed or suggestive rather than cheeky. The expression should communicate “I've got this.”

**Why explore it:** strongest mascot personality and the best opportunity for occasional larger illustrations.

## 04 — Wag Stamp

**Idea:** turn the joke into a simple identifying symbol rather than a full character illustration.

**Composition:** a compact geometric rump silhouette, with two short squared-off paws, softly rounded haunches and a thick comma-like raised tail. Use a single connected filled silhouette wherever practical. Cut the keyhole directly out of the center beneath the tail. Omit head, ears, face, coat markings and fur.

**Drawing:** draw in one color first. Balance straight paw edges against rounded haunches and an asymmetric tail. The tail/body join must be unambiguous. A subtle W-like rhythm may emerge from the two paws and central gap, but do not force a literal W or compromise the dog to create it.

**Color:** cream on plum first; plum on cream second. Add an optional apricot fill only after the single-color shape is convincing. No gradients required.

**Wordmark:** this compact symbol can sit comfortably beside a stronger wordmark. Keep the letters plain enough to give the unusual symbol room. A circular badge is a secondary application, not part of the master shape.

**At small sizes:** aim for the fewest edges and strongest silhouette. Test the keyhole and tail as a 24 px symbol as well as a 40 px icon. If the internal cuts close up, enlarge them rather than adding an outline.

**What could go wrong:** without ears or coat markings it can read as a cat, padlock or abstract blob. Distinguish the dog through a sturdier lower body and tail shape; don't solve it by adding tiny details.

**Why explore it:** strongest prospect for a versatile standalone logo, one-color use, login screen and a restrained alternate icon.

## First presentation and comparison

Render one focused concept for each direction before producing many coat colors. Present them at equal apparent size on the same plum background, with a labeled full-size view and actual 40, 60 and 120 px previews. Include a single-color silhouette and a light-background version for each. Keep breed and palette variants separate from the four composition choices.

Use these as initial drawing targets, not platform requirements:

| Property | Starting target |
| --- | --- |
| Icon source canvas | 1024 × 1024 |
| Main silhouette extent | About 72–80% of canvas; adjust optically for the tail |
| Outer safe margin | Aim for at least 10% on each side |
| Keyhole height | About 16–20% of canvas, approximately 6–8 px in a 40 px preview |
| Keyhole bowl width | About 8–11% of canvas |
| Narrowest essential stroke/gap | Aim for at least 2 px at the 40 px preview size |
| Presentation output | Clean square exports plus separately masked previews |

Judge the designs in this order:

1. Does it read as a dog from behind, without explanation?
2. Does the keyhole register immediately, including at 40 px?
3. Does the joke still work with no name or slogan?
4. Is the silhouette identifiable in one color?
5. Does it feel like a personal photo vault that happens to be funny, rather than a pet tracker or a novelty joke app?

**Recommended pair to develop first:** Little Guardian and Wag Stamp. They compare the expressive mascot against the simplest usable mark. Cheeky Close-Up is the bolder joke; Caught You Looking tests how much extra personality the face provides.
